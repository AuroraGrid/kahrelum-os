Four AI Platforms Failed a Government Safety Test. What Does That Actually Prove?

By Hasan Raza Kazmi · KAHRELUM Intelligence · GAO document October 16th, public release November 18th · Source cutoff November 18th, 2026 at 14:50 UTC

Finding: SUPPORTED for the bounded demonstrated failures; NOT YET VERIFIABLE for current general failure rates and cross-platform rankings.

Four major AI platforms. Four controlled government tests. Four cases in which investigators ultimately obtained information that the systems' safeguards were intended to restrict. The failures are documented. A general failure rate is not.

On November 18th, 2026, the U.S. Government Accountability Office publicly released GAO-27-108814, an investigation of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok. Investigators found that each platform, under sustained adversarial testing, supplied information they assessed as feasible for harmful use.

That is a serious result. It is not the same thing as proving how frequently any platform fails, which developer is safest, or whether its newest models remain vulnerable in precisely the same way.

What the watchdog actually tested

The report is dated October 16th, 2026, and was publicly released on November 18th. The broader audit ran from December 2025 through October 2026, while the central platform interactions occurred in spring 2026.

GAO selected two harmful-use scenarios, one involving threats to public safety and another concerning critical infrastructure. Investigators assigned the four widely used platforms to the scenarios, two platforms per scenario, and anonymized the company-to-scenario mapping in the detailed findings.

The testers used prepared prompts. They reported that initial requests triggered protections, including refusals or other interventions. Continuing interactions eventually produced outputs judged useful to the selected scenarios. In each case the process took less than 12 hours.

That time is an observation about these tests. It is not a promise that an ordinary user, in a different situation or with a current model, could replicate a result on the same timetable. GAO withheld details that might facilitate harmful misuse. This review does not recreate the prompts, targets or procedures.

The denominator that changes the headline

Readers may see “four out of four” and infer a 100% safety-failure rate. That interpretation is not supported. Four platform-and-scenario combinations are not a representative sample of ordinary traffic, and the testing deliberately continued through adversarial interaction to find potential weaknesses.

The design is useful for answering whether a safeguard can fail under the circumstances examined. It cannot estimate the fraction of everyday interactions that fail, nor compare developer reliability on identical tasks.

Imagine a lock tested by a specialist who keeps working until it opens. Successful entry demonstrates a weakness. It does not show how often the lock would fail in normal use or how the specialist's method compares with attempts against other locks.

A comparative estimate would need a defined set of prompts, model versions, tester conditions, attempt counts, clear success criteria and enough observations to support an uncertainty interval. GAO did not claim to have run that study.

What the initial refusals reveal

All four systems demonstrated at least some protective behavior. GAO documented denials and, for certain platforms, content removal or account restrictions. Those protections matter and should not disappear from the story simply because subsequent testing found ways around them.

But an initial refusal is not a comprehensive safety result. Organizations need to examine the full interaction history: when a system stopped, how it handled repeated requests, whether it escalated, and what consequences followed. Neither the first refusal nor the eventual failure should be reported alone.

Feasibility is not a completed attack

The investigators went beyond reading chatbot responses. They consulted subject-matter experts, reviewed locations and conducted limited checks that they considered safe. They concluded that the information could meaningfully assist a malicious actor.

They did not document four realized attacks. And the model outputs were not error-free. In one infrastructure-related case, investigators determined that only two of four proposed locations satisfied relevant feasibility criteria. Some generated maps contained mistakes.

GAO's operational meaning of “viable” concerned the likely usefulness of the information for planning. Actual success would depend on additional conditions. That distinction does not erase the risk, but it prevents fictional certainty from being added to the evidence.

What the developers contested

Anthropic said the testing reflected an earlier version of its safeguards and requested more detail about the models, tiers, timing and resisted attacks. Google described continuing safety improvements. OpenAI pointed to controls and refusals and challenged whether the report adequately separated model output from investigators' outside research. GAO replied that such research reflected how real users could combine AI with other information sources. SpaceXAI sought additional records and described ongoing moderation work.

These responses are relevant counterevidence to any claim that the tests capture every platform's current state. They are not independent demonstrations that every observed weakness has been fixed. A mitigation claim becomes testable when current configurations undergo a new, appropriately documented evaluation.

What the results mean for business AI

This is not only about chatbots. Organizations increasingly rely on AI systems to summarize evidence, suggest actions and sometimes interact with external tools. Risks can emerge from the answer, the permissions granted to an agent, inadequate review or the failure to stop when a source is unreliable.

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework offers a useful principle: define, measure, manage and govern risks throughout a system's lifecycle. GAO's earlier malicious-use review likewise treated safeguards as work requiring ongoing evaluation, not a one-time certification.

KAHRELUM's existing AI Assurance service and methodology separately examine evidence, authorization, contradiction and escalation records. That is a methodological connection, not a claim that KAHRELUM replicated GAO's tests or independently certified the four products.

What would change the assessment?

A new independently controlled evaluation of current versions could establish whether the previously observed weaknesses persist. A larger, standardized test with a disclosed denominator could estimate failure frequency. A meaningful comparison would use consistent conditions across models, while a human-without-AI control group could help measure capability uplift.

Verified mitigation results would narrow the concern. Additional failures under current configurations would strengthen it. Until those findings exist, comparative rankings and general present-day failure probabilities remain unknown.

The assessment

SUPPORTED: GAO demonstrated meaningful safety failures in all four selected platform-and-scenario combinations, including feasible information under controlled adversarial testing.

SUPPORTED: The systems initially responded with protections, and the study was not designed as a statistically comparable general failure-rate survey.

NOT YET VERIFIABLE: Which developer currently has the highest failure rate, whether each vulnerability persists unchanged, or how much these products independently increase malicious actors' capabilities.

The point is not simply that four systems produced dangerous information. It is that safeguards operated, were tested further, and proved insufficient under specific conditions. Preserving both the failures and the limits of inference makes the evidence more useful.
