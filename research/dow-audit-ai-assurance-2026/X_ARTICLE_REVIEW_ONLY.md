# The Pentagon Is Spending $200 Million on AI to Fix Its Audits. Who Audits the AI?

On November 16, the Pentagon announced a financial audit breakthrough.

On the same day, reporting on the Inspector General's work described the ninth consecutive year without a clean **department-wide** audit opinion.

Both descriptions can be true. And the distance between them is the story.

The Department of War says clean audit opinions will cover more than 56% of its assets and over 90% of liabilities, *including component opinions expected in January and February 2027*. Those future opinions matter: expected coverage is not the same thing as an audit already signed.

Meanwhile, the broader financial statements still received a **disclaimer of opinion**, according to contemporaneous reporting. In plain English, the auditors could not obtain sufficient appropriate evidence to say the department-wide statements were fairly presented. That is not proof the money vanished or that anyone stole it. It is a serious unresolved assurance gap.

Now add the technological pivot.

## The $200 million line in the budget

A May 4 funding allocation plan sets aside **$200 million** for AI and automation aimed at accelerating audit work. The plan divides it into five buckets:

- $110 million for automated transaction reconciliation.
- $30 million for gathering evidentiary material for auditors.
- $45 million for Navy automation and AI use cases.
- $10 million for SOC 1 controls-report expansion.
- $5 million for new Advana software capabilities.

This is a **funding allocation**, not proof the money has all been spent. It is not a published open competition for outside vendors.

The most interesting number may be $30 million. It is intended to help assemble the evidence auditors need, replacing an extraordinary amount of manual work.

But there is a distinction every AI buyer should understand:

**Finding a document is not the same as proving that document supports the decision.**

A system can locate a receipt, match a transaction, summarize a discrepancy, and produce a polished audit packet. None of those steps alone establishes that the underlying transaction was valid, the supporting record was contemporaneous, the reconciliation was complete, or an exception was properly escalated.

Automation compresses time. It does not abolish the burden of proof.

## GAO raised the warning months before the announcement

In May, the Government Accountability Office described the Pentagon's revised audit strategy as a shift toward validating material account balances, large-sample testing, and targeted use of AI rather than prioritizing every underlying control deficiency.

GAO recognized the potential for progress. It also warned about unresolved IT weaknesses, fraud risk, audit-remediation transparency, and whether an intensive approach would remain sustainable beyond 2028.

That tension is crucial.

A clean audit opinion is valuable. Strong everyday financial controls are also valuable. They are related goals, but not identical. A department can improve its documented financial position without yet correcting every system that created its original problems.

Nor is the new platform simply untested. The department reported that its War Data Platform received a clean SOC 1 Type 2 controls examination for a defined period. That is meaningful evidence for the controls within that examination's scope. It does **not** certify every AI-generated assertion or deliver a clean agency-wide financial statement opinion.

## The missing test: decision-level evidence integrity

Here are five questions that should follow any deployment of AI in audit evidence workflows.

**1. Provenance.** Can every conclusion be traced to a specific source record, version, and cutoff time?

**2. Completeness.** Does the packet capture the full relevant transaction population, or only the records the system found easiest to match?

**3. Contradictions.** When source systems disagree, does the software surface the dispute or silently choose a convenient value?

**4. Authority.** Which findings can automation resolve, and which require a human reviewer or licensed auditor?

**5. Reproducibility.** Can another authorized reviewer reproduce the result without trusting the model's summary?

Those are not accusations against the Pentagon or any contractor. They are proposed evaluation criteria. The public evidence available here does not establish whether current tools pass or fail them.

## Why the percentages still need auditing

There is also a reporting reconciliation to perform. Reuters quoted 14 of 25 standalone entities passing, representing 56% of assets and 86% of liabilities. Other contemporaneous coverage described 12 completed clean opinions and four additional ones still projected. The department's announcement projects *over 90%* of liabilities after anticipated opinions.

Different cutoff dates, definitions or reporting populations may explain the apparent conflict. Until the signed component reports are compared line by line, those numbers should not be collapsed into one verified achievement.

The lesson is almost too on the nose: **the reporting of audit progress itself needs traceable evidence.**

## The commercial lesson reaches far beyond the Pentagon

Every business deploying AI to approve claims, reconcile invoices, authorize payments, triage compliance alerts, or document exceptions faces a scaled-down version of this same problem.

A model can produce a convincing answer faster than a reviewer can reconstruct how it arrived there.
