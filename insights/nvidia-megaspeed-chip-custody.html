Who can trace the AI chips?

The Nvidia–Megaspeed controversy is not resolved by a photograph, a shipping invoice or a government statement. It turns on whether investigators can reconcile chips, owners, end users and decisions.

November 8, 2026 · Original evidence cutoff November 8 · November 10 and 12, 2026 updates · Case CHIP-01 remains OPEN · Conceptual illustrations are not evidence

Evidence update · November 10, 2026 update

The accountability question moves from reporting to formal process

The November 8 baseline asked where the GPU and intelligence records led. The next phase is now independently documented: Nvidia's board has opened an investigation; Jensen Huang has apologized for an extramarital affair; a reported shareholder lawsuit challenges disclosure; and congressional scrutiny has a fixed response deadline. These developments do not establish espionage, a security breach, an unlawful chip shipment or securities fraud.

Verified corporate action · Nov 8

Independent board investigation

Nvidia's independent directors, advised by outside independent counsel, announced a review into allegations of foreign-intelligence targeting. The board says it has no reason to believe secrets or national security were compromised and expresses confidence in Huang. It has not published a completed investigative finding. Primary board statement.

Verified direct statement · Nov 8

Infidelity acknowledged, espionage unproven

Huang publicly acknowledged infidelity, apologized to his family and coworkers, and committed to cooperating with the investigation. His own published message does not name a partner or concede intelligence compromise. Personal conduct is not proof of an intelligence operation.

Official rebuttal · Nov 9

China disputes the reported claims

Spokesperson Guo Jiakun characterized the Chinese-spy claim as unsubstantiated and a smear. This is a first-party denial and must be kept beside U.S.-sourced allegations, not presented as a finding from independent investigators. Foreign Ministry transcript.

Reported litigation · Nov 9

New proposed shareholder class action

Reuters reports that shareholders led by Mark Kistenmacher sued Nvidia and Huang in San Jose federal court over alleged nondisclosure of material risks. A class action complaint is an allegation, not an adjudication; its court docket has not been independently authenticated for this update. Reuters filing report.

What changed in the market and Washington

Market: NVDA closed November 9 at $178.67, down 4.27% from its previous close. The trading day also featured wider AI-sector pressures; one price movement cannot establish a single causal mechanism. Historical prices.

Executive branch: President Trump said he spoke to Huang and that Huang denied knowing the woman was a spy. Trump also said the FBI was examining the issue. Those are Trump's remarks as reported by Reuters, not an independently verified FBI statement or finding. Reuters report.

Public events: TechCrunch reports that Huang withdrew from planned ElevenLabs and Anthropic appearances. This changes the public calendar, not the confirmed CEO position. TechCrunch.

Policy: Reuters identified the AI Overwatch Act, Chip Security Act and Match Act as potential FY2027 defense-bill provisions affecting exports, tracking and allied restrictions. None is treated here as an enacted consequence of CHIP-01. Reuters legislative coverage.

New evidence · November 12, 2026 · Attributed company response

Megaspeed says it cooperated with BIS inspectors and commissioned a GPU inventory review

Reuters reported on November 12 that Megaspeed CEO and Chairman James Tan said his firm had cooperated with inspections by the U.S. Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS), and stood ready to cooperate further. Tan said Megaspeed retained an American law firm to lead a comprehensive GPU census and independently assess its export-control compliance practices. Retaining a firm is not the same as receiving a finished independent audit.

What this does not establish: No independent BIS inspection conclusion, completed law-firm inventory or serial-level custody audit was verified for this update. The corporate account does not itself establish either illegal diversion or exoneration, nor does it prove an intelligence relationship or knowing wrongdoing by Nvidia or its CEO. The original November 8 investigation, November 10 evidence addendum and unscored leadership forecast remain unchanged. CHIP-01 remains OPEN.

Three short-term checkpoints, and what they can actually prove

  1. November 11: Congressional chairs asked Huang to accept a November 18 session or offer an alternative. A request is not a subpoena. House request.
  2. November 17: Nvidia's scheduled FY27 Q3 earnings. Watch for a dated board or disclosure update, without assuming one will be provided. Official event calendar.
  3. November 18: Requested closed-door House briefing. As of this November 10 cutoff, whether it will occur is unknown. Committee confirmation of request.

The governing research questions remain separate: access, leakage, GPU custody, beneficial ownership, export licenses and disclosure duty. No public finding establishes knowing participation in espionage. Both the dated event and falsification log and Nvidia/Megaspeed/Singapore contrary evidence must survive future revisions.

Important distinction: The reported intelligence allegations are contested. This article does not establish that Jensen Huang knowingly cooperated with a spy, that Megaspeed illegally exported chips, or that U.S. officials knowingly protected wrongdoing.

Start with the government record, not the rumor

On November 5, three U.S. House committee chairmen publicly requested a closed-door briefing from Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang concerning his interactions with former Megaspeed executive Alice Huang and Nvidia's foreign-espionage safeguards. They proposed November 18 and requested acceptance or another proposed date by November 11. The letter is a congressional oversight action, not a finding of guilt. [1]

In a separate official statement on October 31, Singapore's Ministry of Home Affairs said that investigations into Megaspeed are ongoing. Crucially, it also denied that Singapore had conducted the alleged investigation into Alice Huang's Chinese intelligence ties described by the Wall Street Journal. That denial must be preserved even when reporting from U.S. officials is taken seriously. [2]

What the intelligence reporting says, and what it does not

The Financial Times reported November 7 that an intelligence-based memorandum alleged a romantic relationship between Jensen Huang and Alice Huang, whom U.S. intelligence reportedly assessed as having been recruited by China's Ministry of State Security. The New York Times separately reported a suspected operation involving access to the Nvidia chief executive and the company's China policy. The underlying memorandum, surveillance and individual intelligence sources have not been publicly produced. Their existence as reported does not independently authenticate the assertions they contain. [3] [4]

Nvidia has described the interactions as ordinary commercial meetings, denied sharing sensitive trade-secret or national-security information with regional cloud customers, and said its reviews found no unlawful diversion by Megaspeed. Jensen Huang told Bloomberg he had counted the company's chip deployments and described the relationship as normal. Those statements are relevant contrary evidence, not conclusive external audits. [9]

The custody puzzle: 136,000 versus about 86,000

Customs-derived reporting

At least 136,000 GPUs

Bloomberg's late-2025 analysis estimated substantial Nvidia hardware imports by Megaspeed, valued at more than $4.6 billion through November 2025.

Data-center inspection reporting

About 86,000 GPUs

Bloomberg separately described the quantity Nvidia had cataloged at selected data-center operators, with other equipment held in warehouses.

The numerical difference is not a verified count of stolen or missing chips. The two figures reflect different inventory scopes, dates and measurement methods. A credible reconciliation needs shipment IDs, part numbers, serial identifiers, warehouse releases, installation records, facility confirmations, and customer service logs. Analysts should not infer illegal diversion from incompatible denominators. [5]

Bloomberg also identified questions about Megaspeed's earlier corporate structure and substantial later hardware procurement. End-2023 cash of approximately $5.7 million cannot, by itself, determine the sources of financing in 2024–2026. The open questions concern loans, guarantees, related parties, beneficial ownership, and whether transactions satisfied applicable screening requirements. [5]

What BIS's rules actually cover

The U.S. government's May 2025 decision not to enforce the new AI Diffusion Rule did not eliminate every advanced-chip export restriction. BIS's May 2026 clarification states that specified controlled advanced computing items supplied to entities headquartered in China or other Country Group D:5 jurisdictions, or entities with an ultimate parent headquartered there, remain subject to relevant license requirements in third countries, unless an exception applies. The legal test depends on the shipment, classification, ultimate ownership, end user and end use. [6]

Separately, BIS's January 2026 H200 rule moved eligible China-bound license applications to case-by-case review under conditions. That is not universal approval of H200 shipments, and it is not a general authorization to divert restricted Blackwell systems to China. Renting overseas computing services is not identical to physically importing the GPUs, although particular end-user and end-use restrictions can still matter. [7]

Why “the U.S. did nothing” is an unsupported conclusion

The New York Times reported, citing administration officials, that the Commerce Department's Megaspeed inquiry had been passed to the Department of Justice during 2026. There is not yet a public Megaspeed indictment or authenticated referral document in the records reviewed here. This reported handoff must be treated as a lead, neither ignored nor upgraded into a confirmed prosecution. [4]

U.S. prosecutors described dummy servers staged to mislead inspection teams in a March 2026 chip-diversion indictment involving different defendants. It shows why an onsite inventory visit can require independent serial and custody checks. That case is not established as connected to Megaspeed. [8]

The evidence that could settle the dispute

  1. Corporate control: Certified Singapore and Malaysian filings, beneficial-owner attestations, voting rights, credit agreements and guarantees.
  2. Physical hardware: Shipment-by-shipment serial and SKU reconciliation against warehouse receipts, imports, reexports and operating facilities.
  3. Customers and workloads: Confirmed contractual end users, cloud access controls and end-use screening, with lawful confidentiality protections.
  4. Government decision path: The reported DOJ referral, dated intelligence dissemination list, BIS licensing determinations and official communications with Nvidia.
  5. Adversarial review: Original findings plus Nvidia and Megaspeed responses, Singapore's correction, and any contrary inspection evidence.

Each item can change the conclusion in either direction. A chain of custody that closes cleanly would weaken physical-diversion theories. Verified unlawful reexports, false ownership attestations or documented knowing coordination would strengthen them. Policy advocacy predating the alleged intelligence contact is not proof of successful influence.

KAHRELUM finding, November 8: There is substantive, dated reason to investigate the Nvidia–Megaspeed chip supply chain, including reported customs and inventory discrepancies, ongoing Singapore investigation, a reported U.S. DOJ referral and congressional oversight. The available public evidence does not prove spying, physical diversion or deliberate regulatory complicity. Follow the CHIP-01 source register and open questions.
