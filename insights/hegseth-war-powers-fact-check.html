KAHRELUM Insights · War powers · Legal evidence audit

A military lawyer called Hegseth's impeachment a sham. His own sources undercut him.

Eight allegations. One sweeping legal rebuttal. A genuine CRS report whose explicit caveat changes the argument. KAHRELUM compares claims, laws and courts, while preserving its original 76% House-impeachment forecast.

The central evidentiary contradiction

Dr. Brian L. Cox argued that the War Powers Resolution has always been unconstitutional and relied on a Congressional Research Service catalog of hundreds of deployments since 1798. The report accurately says that the United States made eleven formal declarations of war covering five wars. But the same report explicitly warns that inclusion of a deployment does not establish its legality. [2]

The source further says most major undeclared wars in its list, except Korea, received congressional authorization in some form short of a declaration. Thus, a history containing few formal declarations is not proof that Congress lacked meaningful legal power over the use of force.

What the report can establish: past deployments and their descriptions. What it cannot establish by itself: that each deployment was lawful, or that the entire War Powers Resolution is unconstitutional.

Presidential opposition is not constitutional invalidation

A second CRS report (R42699) notes that every president since 1973 has taken the position that the War Powers Resolution infringes on commander-in-chief authority. This is an important fact about executive-branch practice, but an assertion of unconstitutionality by presidents is not the same as a binding judgment invalidating the statute. [3]

The provisions also must be considered separately. Section 5(c)'s concurrent-resolution withdrawal mechanism faces a strong objection in light of the Supreme Court's legislative-veto holding in INS v. Chadha. Section 5(b)'s sixty-day clock, with a possible thirty-day withdrawal extension, presents a different question. Other provisions include consultation and reporting requirements. The entire Act cannot responsibly be dismissed as legally nonexistent solely because presidents have protested it. [4]

Neither does this establish that Massie's war-powers counts are legally correct. The constitutional division of war powers and which military activities trigger specific statutory deadlines remain disputed. A responsible analysis must articulate both competing interpretations.

Two primary sources that cannot be waved away

Federal statute

10 U.S.C. §184

The U.S. Code directs that the Secretary of Defense operate the Civilian Protection Center of Excellence, with functions spanning civilian-harm information, guidance, reporting and lessons learned. This defeats any categorical suggestion that civilian-protection infrastructure is exclusively optional executive policy. It does not establish which policy changes, if any, violated this section. [5]

Federal court · Preliminary injunction

Kelly v. Hegseth

On February 12, 2026, Judge Richard Leon granted a preliminary injunction on Senator Mark Kelly's First Amendment claim. The court found a likelihood of success and criticized the government's treatment of a retired officer serving in Congress. The government appealed. A partial preliminary ruling is not a final adjudication, but it is serious contrary legal evidence. [6]

The maritime targeting leap

The viral rebuttal treats the executive's assertion of a noninternational armed conflict as if it settles the status of everyone allegedly moving drugs on boats. International humanitarian law requires more. According to the ICRC, a noninternational armed conflict involves sufficiently organized groups and hostilities of sufficient intensity, and direct participation in hostilities turns on conduct, not simply labels or political affiliation. [7]

Even if an armed conflict legally exists, individual targeting, precautions, surrender, rescue and treatment of survivors remain separate questions. This is not a finding that every attack was unlawful; it is an objection to presenting legality as automatic.

Impeachment and removal are different events

Massie introduced a privileged House resolution on November 9. House leaders could permit a direct vote, or lawmakers could vote to table or refer it. On November 9, Roll Call reported that Massie had not secured assurances of Republican support. Meanwhile, Democratic leaders signaled support and only a handful of Republicans could determine the outcome depending on attendance. [8]

The House impeaches by adopting an article with a simple majority. A Senate conviction and removal require two-thirds of senators present. A pending impeachment resolution is not itself an impeachment; impeachment is not automatically removal. Nor does disagreeing with a legal defense prove the articles will pass.

These vote mechanics, narrow margins, possible GOP defections, Senate threshold, and competing White House incentives explain why the original KAHRELUM forecast estimated 76% House impeachment but only 5% Senate conviction within the specified deadlines. See the original forecast's definitions and uncertainties rather than interpreting this as a legal guilt score.

The fairest finding

Cox is correct to identify substantial constitutional concerns regarding presidential war powers and particularly the concurrent-resolution withdrawal device. Massie must still substantiate each allegation and its significance as an abuse of office. But Cox has not demonstrated that all eight articles are facially worthless. His source selection omits crucial qualifications, a specific statute and a federal judicial ruling.

The difference between advocacy and an evidence audit is what happens to the strongest contrary evidence. It must stay in the record even when it complicates the headline.
